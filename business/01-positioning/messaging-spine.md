# Messaging spine

The reusable language. One claim, one question, six categories, three commercial
facts, and the words that carry them.

[`positioning-statement.md`](positioning-statement.md) decides what n.abl is.
This file decides how it gets said, in each of the places it has to be said. If
you are writing a web page, an email, a proposal cover, a voicemail or a stand
banner, start here rather than from a blank page.

Nothing in this file is on the website yet. The live site is still organised
around Innovation / Automation / Optimisation.

Last substantive revision: 2026-08-15.

---

## 1. The spine

Five things, in this order. Everything else is decoration.

| # | The element | The line |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The claim** | Technology implementation partner for small businesses |
| 2 | **The question** | "What are you trying to improve?" |
| 3 | **The six categories** | Save time, reduce mistakes, understand your data, build something new, train your team, fix something |
| 4 | **The three commercial facts** | No retainers. Priced on what it saves, not on hours. You own it. |
| 5 | **The honest position on AI** | AI is one tool among several. If the answer is not AI, you get the boring version and pay less. |

A piece of copy is on-spine if it can be traced back to one of those five. If it
cannot, it is filler and it comes out.

**Order matters.** The claim before the question, the question before the
categories, the categories before the price. Leading with price makes us a
quote. Leading with AI makes us the thing we have deliberately stopped being.

## 2. The claim, at four lengths

### Ten seconds, spoken

> "We're a technology implementation partner for small businesses. We find the
> bit of your business that costs the most time or goes wrong the most, and we
> build the right fix for it."

### One line, written

> n.abl is a technology implementation partner for small businesses. We find the
> expensive, repetitive or fragile part of your business and build the right fix
> for it.

### Thirty seconds, spoken

> "We work with small businesses around Nottingham and Alcester. Most of them
> have one job that eats a day a week, or one thing that keeps going wrong and
> costs them every time it does.
>
> We work out what that costs now, what it would cost fixed, and we build the fix
> for a fraction of the first year of the difference. No retainer, you own what
> we build, and if the answer turns out to be forty lines of ordinary code rather
> than anything clever, that is what you get and you pay less for it."

### The standard paragraph, written

The version in section 1 of [`positioning-statement.md`](positioning-statement.md).
Use that wording, not a fresh one written for the occasion.

## 3. The organising question

> **What are you trying to improve?**

This is the structure of the website navigation, the opening of every discovery
call, and the contents page of every proposal. The same six answers in all three
places, deliberately, so a client can always tell where they are.

| Category | Their words | The reply that opens it up |
|---|---|---|
| Save time | "This takes us ages every month" | "How many hours, and who does it?" |
| Reduce mistakes | "We keep getting this wrong" | "When did it last go wrong, and what did that cost?" |
| Understand your data | "We have the data, but not the answers" | "Where does it live now, and who needs the answer?" |
| Build something new | "We need a thing that does X" | "What do you do today instead of having it?" |
| Train your team | "We pay for this and nobody uses it" | "What do you pay for monthly? All of it." |
| Fix something | "It worked and now it doesn't" | "When did it last work, and what changed?" |

Never make a client guess which category they are in. Ask the question, listen to
the sentence, and place it yourself.

## 4. Words we use

Problem. Process. Hours. Mistakes. Cost. Build. Fix. Own. Once. Credits.
Appropriate. Boring. Reliable. Show you the arithmetic. Diagnosis. Scope. Small
first.

Concrete nouns and plain verbs. Numbers wherever a number exists.

## 5. Words we do not use

Solutions provider. Digital transformation. Unlock. Empower. Leverage. Seamless.
Cutting-edge. Game-changing. Revolutionise. Synergy. Journey. Ecosystem.
Best-in-class. World-class. Passionate. Bespoke, which is overused locally to the
point of meaninglessness. "Partner with us on your AI journey", which is four
banned words in one clause.

**Also avoid "agency".** It sets the wrong frame, invites a retainer question,
and puts us back in the category we deliberately left.

**Avoid "solution" as a noun** in client-facing copy where "the fix", "the
system" or "the thing we build" will do. It survives internally as a pricing
term (efficiency solutions, capability solutions) because it is precise there.

**Never write a percentage or a statistic we cannot source.** No "clients
typically see", no "most businesses waste", no invented benchmarks. The
arithmetic we show is the client's own numbers, which is stronger anyway because
they can check it.

## 6. Sentence patterns that work

**Lead with their cost, not our capability.**
> "That is about a day a week. What is the person doing it paid?"

**Name the boring answer before they suspect an upsell.**
> "This does not need AI. It needs a form and a database."

**Give the price a shape they can check.**
> "Roughly £1,200 once, against about £2,400 a year of labour."

**Close ownership before it is asked.**
> "You own it. If you never speak to us again, it keeps working."

**Say the risk first.**
> "The honest risk here is that we scope it too big and it overruns. So let us
> not. Pick the worst single part."

**Offer the cheaper answer out loud.**
> "If the real answer is that nobody was shown how to use the software, that is a
> training day, not a project, and it costs you a lot less."

**Use their unit, not ours.**
Vans, jobs, quotes, certificates, tickets, pallets, patients, sites. Never
"instances", "records" or "workflows" in a first conversation.

---

## 7. The three commercial facts, said in the client's language

**No retainers.**
> "You do not pay us monthly to be on standby. You buy support when you actually
> need support."

**Priced on what it saves, not on hours.**
> "If I charge by the day, I get paid more for being slow, and you have no idea
> what the final number is until it arrives. So we price it against what the
> problem costs you, and I show you the sum."

**You own it.**
> "The code, the data, the accounts. In your name from the start where possible.
> Nothing switches off, and I do not hold anything hostage. The only thing you
> stop getting is me."

These three are the differentiators that cost nothing to say and are hardest for
a competitor to copy. Say all three in the first conversation, unprompted.

## 8. The honest position on AI

Said once, early, and then not laboured:

> "AI is one of the tools we use. It is not the point. If the best answer to your
> problem is forty lines of ordinary code, you get forty lines of ordinary code,
> and you pay less for it."

And the line that builds trust faster than anything else in the pitch:

> "If AI is not the right answer for your problem, I will tell you, and I will
> build the boring version instead. It will be cheaper and it will break less
> often."

**Two things never to say**, because they are wrong and someone will eventually
check:

1. That we "run Claude locally". Claude Code is a local interface and
   orchestration environment; the Claude models themselves are cloud-hosted. What
   is genuinely local is the small open-weight models on our own machines. Say
   "some of this runs on a model on our own hardware and nothing leaves" only
   when that is true of the specific build being discussed.
2. That data is "completely secure" or "never used for training" as a blanket
   assurance. Say what happens, specifically, for their build. Most of what we
   ship touches no model at all, which is the more reassuring fact anyway.

## 9. Proof, and the honest way to talk about it

n.abl is new. There are no case studies, no client names and no testimonials, and
we do not invent any. What we do have is real:

- **The software we built for ourselves.** A marketing site, a client portal with
  access-key sign-in and row-level security, an internal team space, a sales CRM,
  six branded email templates and a document generator. Built, deployed and in
  use. It is honest to describe that as a portfolio, and it can be demonstrated.
- **Security decisions we can point at.** Portal access keys are 59.5-bit and
  generated with a cryptographically secure random number generator. Login is
  rate limited per IP, per key prefix and globally. Storage is private and served
  through short-lived signed URLs. Fonts are self-hosted rather than loaded from
  a font CDN, so visiting the site does not disclose a visitor's IP address to a
  third party.
- **The arithmetic.** We will price a job in front of a client, on paper, and let
  them check it. Most people quoting them for technology will not.

The reusable paragraph:

> "I am not going to pretend to a client list I do not have. n.abl is new. What I
> can show you is what I have built, which is the software this business runs on,
> and you can judge the work rather than the reference. And I would rather be
> judged on the diagnosis anyway."

The full script is objection 6 in [`objection-handling.md`](objection-handling.md).

## 10. Channel patterns

Skeletons, not templates. Fill them with the specific business.

### Cold email

Four sentences, in this order. Under 120 words.

1. **The specific observation.** Something from their own website, taken from
   research, that shows this was not sent to a thousand people. "Your credit
   account form is a PDF that has to be printed and emailed back."
2. **The consequence, as a question, not an assertion.** "I would guess somebody
   retypes those into your system."
3. **What we do, in one line.** The short claim.
4. **A small ask.** Twenty minutes, not a meeting about a partnership.

Plus: who we are, where we are, and a plain opt-out line. Identity and opt-out
requirements apply to every message, and the sending path hard-blocks opted-out
records at the database level.

**Nothing is sent without the two human approval gates** in the master plan,
section 5. Research is automated first. Sending is automated last.

### Voicemail, under twenty seconds

> "Morning, it's [name] from n.abl, we're a small technology firm in
> [Nottingham/Alcester]. I was looking at your site and had one specific question
> about how your quotes get put together. Worth two minutes if you get a chance:
> [number]. Thanks."

### Opening a first call

> "Thanks for the time. I am going to be useless if I talk, so I would rather
> ask: what are you trying to improve? What is the thing that annoys you most
> about how the work gets done at the moment?"

Then stay quiet. The first answer is the category.

### Website page structure

The six categories are the navigation. Each page: their sentence as the heading,
what it usually turns out to be, what we build, how it is priced, and what we
would tell them not to bother with. The last section is unusual, and it is the
reason the page is believable.

### Proposal contents

1. What you told me the problem was, in your words.
2. What it costs now, with the arithmetic.
3. What we will build.
4. What it will cost, and what that leaves you.
5. What is not included.
6. What happens afterwards, and what you own.

Section 5 is not optional. An unstated exclusion becomes an expectation.

## 11. Brand rules that affect copy

Full detail in `business/02-brand/`. Three rules matter to anyone writing:

1. **The name is lower case with a full stop: `n.abl`.** The full stop is a
   square and it is amber. Do not capitalise it to "N.abl" at the start of a
   sentence. Rewrite the sentence instead.
2. **The logo is drawn artwork, not type.** A 13-unit monoline stroke with butt
   caps and true-circle curves, mastered at `public/brand/wordmark.svg` and
   `public/brand/mark.svg`. Never write that the wordmark is "set in" any
   typeface, in any document, ever. The old pack says exactly that, and the old
   pack is wrong in every particular: warm espresso `#0E0C0A` and amber
   `#E9AC57`, not black and lime.
3. **On light grounds, amber accent text must be the deep amber `#B87718`.** The
   primary amber `#E9AC57` reaches only 1.97:1 on near-white and fails contrast
   outright. This applies to proposals, PDFs and slides, not only to the website.

Typefaces are Space Grotesk for display, Inter Tight for interface, JetBrains
Mono for keys and figures, all self-hosted rather than requested from a font CDN.

## 12. Tone

Plain, direct, confident, quiet. British English. Short paragraphs. Concrete
numbers rather than adjectives. Never breathless.

The test: **would you say this sentence out loud, to a stranger, in a pub, without
feeling like a salesman?** If not, rewrite it. The brand is warm espresso and
amber rather than neon for exactly the same reason the copy is plain. It is meant
to read as competence, not as noise.

## 14. Review

Rewrite this file after the first ten qualified conversations, using the words
those businesses actually used rather than the words we expect them to use. The
lines above are a reasoned starting point. Real ones will be better.

Do not revise because a single prospect did not understand something. Revise when
three do not.
