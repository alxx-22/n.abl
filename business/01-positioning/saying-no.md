# Saying no

The exclusion list, turned into words a founder can actually say on a call.

Knowing what n.abl does not do is easy on paper and hard at half past four on a
Thursday when someone is offering money. This file exists so the no is already
written, and the only thing required in the moment is to read it out in your own
voice.

Time is the only inventory. Every hour spent on work that was never going to fit
is an hour not spent on work that would have.

Last substantive revision: 2026-08-15.

---

## 1. Why this is worth doing well

A slow maybe is the most expensive thing in the business. It costs the hours, it
costs the pipeline honesty, and it usually ends in a bad engagement anyway.

A fast, friendly, well-explained no does three things a maybe cannot:

1. It gives back the hours immediately.
2. It gets remembered. In a two-town territory, being the person who told
   somebody they did not need to spend money is a marketing strategy with a
   longer half-life than any advert.
3. It produces referrals. People who were turned away honestly send other people,
   because the honesty was the memorable part.

**The rule: disqualify cheerfully and early.** Cheerfully is not softness. It is
what makes the no repeatable without dreading it.

---

## 2. The shape of a good no

Four moves, in this order. Every script below is this shape.

1. **Say it plainly, in the first sentence.** Not "I'm not sure we'd be the best
   fit possibly". "I don't think we're the right people for this."
2. **Give the actual reason.** One sentence. The real one, not a diplomatic
   substitute. People can tell, and the real reason is more useful to them.
3. **Point somewhere useful.** The right kind of supplier, the off-the-shelf
   product, the thing they should do instead. If you genuinely do not know, say
   so rather than inventing a direction.
4. **Leave the door open, specifically.** Not "keep in touch". "If the quoting
   side ever becomes the problem, that is exactly our sort of thing."

Then **log it in the CRM with the reason**. A recorded, specific no is an asset:
it stops the same lead being worked twice, and after fifty of them it tells us
which parts of the profile are wrong.

### The generic version

Use this when nothing more specific applies:

> "I don't think we're the right fit for this, and I'd rather say so now than
> take your money and prove it later. What you actually want is [X], and the
> person for that is [Y] if I know one.
>
> If that changes, or if something else comes up that's more our sort of thing,
> do come back."

## 3. Hard nos

These end the pursuit. They are not negotiations.

### 3.1 "We'd like to talk about our AI strategy."

**Why no.** Technology-led rather than problem-led. The requirement is framed as
a tool, so there is nothing to diagnose, no measurable before and after, and no
way to know when it is finished. These engagements end badly for both sides, and
they are the most tempting to accept because they sound well-funded.

**Say:**

> "Honestly, that's not what we do. We don't sell AI strategies, we fix specific
> expensive things, and AI is only one of the tools we'd use to do it.
>
> If you can tell me the one job in the business that eats the most time or goes
> wrong the most often, I'll happily look at that. If what you want is somebody
> to tell you where AI fits across the whole business, that's a consultancy piece
> and it isn't us."

**If they redirect to a real problem, the conversation is back on.** That happens
often enough to be worth the two minutes.

**Never say.** "Yes, we can help with your AI roadmap." It is the old positioning
speaking, and it undoes the repositioning in one sentence.

---

### 3.2 "We only work with suppliers on a monthly retainer."

**Why no.** Structural, not a pricing preference. A retainer pays us whether or
not there is work, which gives us an incentive to keep work going rather than
finish it.

**Say:**

> "Then we're probably not for you, and that's a deliberate choice rather than an
> awkward one.
>
> We don't do retainers because it means you pay every month whether you need us
> or not, and it means I get paid for the work not finishing. What we do instead
> is: you pay once for the build, you own it, and afterwards you can buy a block
> of credits and spend them when you actually need something. If you go six
> months without needing me, that costs you nothing.
>
> If it has to be a monthly invoice, I'd rather tell you now."

**The one thing that is not a retainer.** Staged payments against delivery
milestones are a reasonable accommodation for cash flow and should be offered if
that is the real concern. [PLACEHOLDER — agree a standard payment schedule, for
example a deposit on signature and the balance on delivery, before the first
quote goes out.]

---

### 3.3 "Could you take equity? / Pay you once it's working?"

**Why no.** That is a co-founder or an investor, not a client. It also puts our
income on somebody else's business risk, at a stage where the business cannot
carry that.

**Say:**

> "That's a co-founder conversation rather than a client one, and I'm not looking
> for one of those.
>
> If there's a piece of it small enough to pay for outright, I'll build that
> piece properly and you'll own it. If there isn't, we're better off leaving it."

**Never say.** "Maybe on the next one." It reads as a yes and it comes back.

---

### 3.4 "Our procurement team will send you the questionnaire."

**Why no.** In phase one, the overhead exceeds the deal. A security
questionnaire, a preferred supplier list and a two-signature purchase process are
weeks of unpaid work against a four-figure job.

**Say:**

> "That's usually a sign we're too small for each other, and I'd rather be
> straight about it.
>
> We work with businesses where the person I'm talking to can decide. Once there
> is a procurement process, the paperwork costs more than the job does, and
> you'd end up paying for that somewhere.
>
> If there's a smaller piece that sits outside all that, I'm interested. If not,
> you want a supplier who's set up for it, and that's not us yet."

**Note the "yet".** This is a phase-one constraint, not a permanent one.

---

### 3.5 "We need it to give financial / legal / medical advice."

**Why no.** We build the system. We do not supply the advice inside it. This
boundary goes in the scope document every time, and it is not a formality.

**Say:**

> "I'll build the system. I won't build the advice.
>
> So if you want something that gathers the information, produces the documents,
> tracks the deadlines and puts it in front of your qualified person, that's
> exactly the sort of thing we do. If you want it to make the judgement itself
> and tell a customer what to do, that's a regulated thing and it isn't ours to
> build.
>
> That's not me being cautious. It's whose responsibility it is when it's wrong."

**This is often a yes in disguise.** The system without the advice inside it is
frequently the better product anyway, and saying this clearly tends to increase
trust rather than lose the job.

---

### 3.6 "Head office will need to approve it."

**Why no**, unless head office is in the room. The person in front of us cannot
buy, and the decision will be made by someone who has not heard any of the
diagnosis.

**Say:**

> "Fair enough. Can we get whoever signs it off into the same conversation? I'd
> rather not have you relaying my answers to their questions.
>
> If that's not possible, I'd honestly rather leave it. The bit that's worth
> anything is the diagnosis, and it doesn't survive being passed along."

Same script for a franchisee whose systems are dictated by the franchisor, with
the reason changed: the integration will not be permitted, so the work cannot be
delivered even if it is bought.

---

### 3.7 The business is not viable

**Why no.** Automating a loss-making process makes the loss arrive faster. Signs
to check before the meeting: a formal insolvency process, a winding-up petition,
a pattern of charges registered against the company, a dormant filing at
Companies House while the business is clearly trading.

**Say** (carefully, and without diagnosing their business out loud):

> "Can I ask something blunt first? If this job disappeared tomorrow and cost you
> nothing, would the business be comfortable?
>
> The reason I ask is that this kind of work only pays for itself if the
> underlying business is sound. If money is tight right now, spending it here is
> not what I'd advise, and I'd rather say that than sell you something."

**Never say** anything that reads as a judgement on their solvency. Ask the
question, listen, and step back if the answer is what you suspected.

---

### 3.8 "Can you look after our IT? / Our network's a mess."

**Why no.** We do not manage networks, supply hardware, run helpdesks or take
over a Microsoft 365 tenancy. It is a different business with different
economics, and it is a retainer by nature.

**Say:**

> "That's not us. We don't do networks, machines, email accounts or support
> desks, and you genuinely want someone local who does, because when it breaks
> you need them that morning.
>
> What we do is the bit your IT company will tell you isn't their job: the
> process side. The spreadsheet that's become a system, the two products that
> don't talk to each other, the job that eats a day a week. If that's also going
> on, I'm interested in that half."

**Never criticise the incumbent IT provider.** They have the client's ear and
they are a referral source, not a competitor.

---

### 3.9 "Can you get us a list of everyone in [sector] and email them?"

**Why no.** Google's Maps terms restrict using Maps content to create or augment
business listings, mailing lists or telemarketing lists. Beyond the terms, a
machine that can send 10,000 bad emails is a liability, not an asset, and
building one for a client hands them the liability.

**Say:**

> "Not in that shape, no. Scraping a map and emailing everyone on it breaks the
> terms of the source, and it burns your domain and your reputation at the same
> time. Neither comes back cheaply.
>
> What I will build is the version that works: proper research on a small number
> of businesses that actually fit, a record of where each one came from, a
> genuine opt-out that the system enforces rather than remembers, and a human
> approving what goes out. It's slower to set up and it's the only version I'd
> put my name on.
>
> If you want volume rather than that, I'm the wrong supplier."

**The compliance part is not negotiable and it is part of the price.** The record
carries `subscriber_type`, `lawful_basis`, `source`, `source_date`,
`privacy_notice_status`, `marketing_status`, `opt_out`, `suppression_list` and
`contact_history`, and the sending path hard-blocks opted-out records at the
database level.

---

### 3.10 Outside both territories

**Why no**, during phase one. Concentration produces referrals, recognisable
names and a reputation that travels between businesses that drink in the same
places. A scattered client list produces none of that. This will feel wasteful
the first time a good lead is turned away.

**Say:**

> "We're deliberately only working around [Nottingham / Alcester] at the moment,
> because being able to sit in someone's office for an hour is most of what makes
> this work. Doing it remotely for you would be a worse job than you deserve.
>
> If you know anyone in either patch with the same problem, I'd genuinely
> appreciate the introduction. And if we open the area up, I'll come back to you
> first."

---

### 3.11 "We've got a developer in-house, they'd just need a hand."

**Why no.** They will, correctly, do it themselves, and the engagement becomes an
argument about approach with someone whose job it is to disagree.

**Say:**

> "Then you don't need me. Honestly. Anything I'd build, they'll build closer to
> the business and maintain it afterwards.
>
> Where I'd be useful is if there's something they've had on the list for a year
> and will never get to, and you'd rather it just existed. That's a clean piece
> of work with a start and a finish. Otherwise, save your money."

## 4. Soft nos: proceed only with eyes open

These are not automatic refusals. They are conditions on a yes.

### 4.1 The job is too small to be worth doing

Sometimes the measured value is under about £1,000 a year and there is no job in
it. Saying so is worth more than the job would have been.

> "I've done the sum and I don't think this is worth paying for. It's about an
> hour a month, so call it £240 a year, and anything I build is going to cost
> more than that to be worth building.
>
> What I'd do instead is [the twenty-minute suggestion]. If you want, I'll show
> you on the call and it costs you nothing.
>
> The bigger version of this problem, if it ever turns up, is the one to ring me
> about."

Give the free advice. It costs twenty minutes and it is the most reliable way
this business gets remembered.

### 4.2 A committee

More than two people who each need to agree, in a business under fifty people,
usually means nobody has authority.

> "Before we go further, who actually decides this? Not who's involved, who
> decides. If it's three people who all need to agree, I'd rather get all three
> in a room once than do this three times and lose something in each telling."

If a third person appears who was never mentioned, revisit the decision-access
score in [`ideal-customer-profile.md`](ideal-customer-profile.md). That is the
failure mode the score exists to catch.

### 4.3 A previous failed project

Not fatal, and often the reason they will talk to a smaller supplier. But the
first conversation has to surface it and the scope has to be smaller than
instinct suggests.

> "Tell me what happened last time. Genuinely, the whole story, including the
> part where it was your fault if it was.
>
> Whatever we do first, I want it smaller than feels sensible. One real thing
> that works, that you can see inside a fortnight."

**Walk away if** the previous supplier is still involved, or if the failure is
described entirely as somebody else's fault with no reflection at all.

### 4.4 Special category or clinical data at the centre of the process

Workable, but the data protection obligations are real, the scope document has to
be right and the schema has to be built properly.

> "This is doable, but it's the kind of data where the paperwork matters as much
> as the build, and I'd want to be straight with you about that before we start
> rather than half way through."

Do not take this as an early engagement while working practices are still being
established.

### 4.5 A finished specification and a request for a price only

Lower value, no diagnosis, and usually quoted against three other suppliers on
price alone.

> "I can price that, but you'd be buying the cheapest pair of hands rather than
> the useful bit. Can I ask what the spec is trying to fix? If I agree with it,
> I'll quote it happily. If I think there's a smaller and cheaper way to get the
> same outcome, I'd rather tell you that first."

Deprioritise if they decline. Log it and move on.

## 5. Saying no during an engagement

Most of the money is lost after the yes, not before it.

### 5.1 Scope creep

> "Yes, we can do that, and it's outside what we agreed, so it's either a change
> to the price or it goes on the list for afterwards. Which would you prefer?
>
> I'm telling you now rather than at the end, because the version where I quietly
> absorb it is the version where the whole thing runs late."

Say it the first time it happens. The first unchallenged "and could it also..."
sets the rule for the rest of the project.

### 5.2 A discount request

> "The price is the price for that scope, but the scope isn't fixed. Tell me your
> budget and I'll tell you honestly what I can do properly inside it. If the
> answer is nothing worth having, I'll tell you that too."

**Reduce scope, not price.** Discounting on request teaches the client the first
price was not real and makes every future price a negotiation.

### 5.3 A free pilot or unpaid proof of concept

> "Yes to a trial, in the sense that the first job should be small enough to be
> its own trial. No to building something for free.
>
> Pick the most annoying single piece. That gets built properly, for a price we
> agree, and it's yours whether or not you ever use me again."

### 5.4 A deadline that cannot be met

> "I can't do that date and do it properly. I can do [date], or I can do a
> smaller version of it by yours. I'd rather give you the choice than the
> optimistic answer."

### 5.5 When it is our fault

> "That one's on me. I've fixed it, it hasn't cost you anything, and here's what
> I've changed so it doesn't happen again."

A defect in something we built and delivered is fixed at our cost, not against
the client's credits. Be generous at the edges of that boundary. Arguing with a
client about whose fault it is costs more than the fix.

## 6. Things never to say when saying no

- **"We're too busy at the moment."** It is a lie unless it is true, and it
  invites them back in three weeks with the same unsuitable job.
- **"Let me think about it and come back to you."** If the answer is no, the
  delay only makes the no worse.
- **"I'll send you some information."** A no dressed as admin. They wait, we feel
  guilty, nothing improves.
- **"That's not really our area"**, with no follow-up. True but useless. Say what
  is our area.
- **Anything critical of another supplier**, including the one who built the
  thing that failed. It reads as insecurity and the local market is small.
- **"For you, I could do it cheaper."** Not a no, and it destroys the price.
- **A vague maybe of any kind.** The whole point of this file.

---

## 7. Log it

Every no goes in the CRM with:

- the reason, in a sentence, using the real reason
- which disqualifier it maps to, from
  [`ideal-customer-profile.md`](ideal-customer-profile.md) section 6
- whether it is permanent or conditional, and on what condition
- a date, if there is a genuine reason to revisit

A record set to `disqualified` with a written reason stops the lead being worked
twice, and it is the raw material for correcting the profile. If fifty nos are
all the same reason, either the sourcing is wrong or the profile is.
