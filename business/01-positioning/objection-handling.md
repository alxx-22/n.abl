# Objection handling

What people will say, what they usually mean, and what to say back.

This is a working script, not a theory of persuasion. The quoted answers are
meant to be said more or less as written, in a normal voice. Adapt the words,
keep the structure.

Last substantive revision: 2026-08-15.

---

## Principles

Five rules that matter more than any individual answer below.

**1. An objection is information, not resistance.** Somebody who says "that seems
expensive" is still in the conversation. Somebody with no objections is usually
being polite on their way out.

**2. Name the risk before they do.** Whoever raises the problem first owns the
frame. If we say "the honest risk here is that we scope this too big and it
overruns", the client stops looking for the catch. If they say it first, we are
defending.

**3. Never argue with a number.** If a client says a price is too high, the
answer is never "it is not". It is either a smaller scope, or the arithmetic
again, or a cheerful no.

**4. Disqualify cheerfully and early.** Time is the only inventory. A fast,
friendly, well-explained no is worth more than a slow maybe, and it is
remembered. In a two-town territory, being remembered as the person who told
someone they did not need to spend money is a marketing strategy.

**5. Never invent proof.** No case studies, no client names, no testimonials, no
statistics we cannot source. This is a hard rule and objection 6 is the one that
tests it.

**Where this file ends.** These twenty are objections from prospects worth
keeping. The ones that end a conversation are in [`saying-no.md`](saying-no.md),
written out as scripts. If an objection turns out to be a disqualifier, stop
handling it and go there.

## Price and commercial

### 1. "How much do you charge? What is your day rate?"

**What it usually means.** They are trying to compare us to a contractor or an
agency, because that is the only shape of technology supplier they have met.

**Say:**

> "We do not work on a day rate. If I charge by the day, I get paid more for
> being slow, and you have no idea what the final number is until it arrives.
>
> What we do instead is work out what the problem costs you now, work out what it
> will cost after it is fixed, and price the job as a fraction of the first year
> of that difference. So before you commit to anything, you will see the
> arithmetic and you will be able to check it.
>
> To do that I need twenty minutes on what the job actually involves. Can you
> walk me through it?"

**Do not say.** Any number before the diagnosis. A range invented on the spot
becomes the anchor for the whole engagement, and it will be wrong.

**Disqualifier?** No. But if they insist on a day rate after two attempts, they
are buying a contractor, not an outcome, and the engagement will be measured in
hours forever.

---

### 2. "That seems like a lot of money."

**What it usually means.** Any of three quite different things, and the answer
depends entirely on which. Find out before responding.

- It is more than they have.
- It is more than they expected, because they were comparing it to a piece of
  software rather than to the labour it removes.
- They do not believe it will work.

**Say, first:**

> "That is fair. Can I ask which part of it feels high — the number itself, or
> what you are getting for it?"

**Then, if it is the comparison:**

> "The way to look at it is against the twelve hours a month, not against a
> software subscription. Twelve hours at what that person costs you is about
> £240 a month, £2,880 a year. This is a one-off. By about month six it has paid
> for itself, and after that it is just gone as a cost."

**If it is genuinely more than they have:**

> "Then let us do less. What is the single worst part of it? We can build that
> piece properly for less, and you can decide about the rest once you have seen
> whether I am any good."

**Do not say.** "I can do you a deal." Discounting on request teaches the client
that the first price was not real, and it makes every future price a negotiation.
Reduce scope, not price.

**Disqualifier?** Only if the smallest useful scope is still out of reach. Then
say so kindly and leave the door open.

---

### 3. "Can you do it cheaper?"

**What it usually means.** Testing whether the price was real. In many trades,
asking for a discount is simply good manners.

**Say:**

> "The price is the price for that scope, but the scope is not fixed. Tell me
> what your budget is and I will tell you honestly what I can do properly inside
> it — and if the answer is nothing worth having, I will tell you that too."

**Do not say.** Yes.

**Disqualifier?** No. It is usually a good sign: people do not haggle over things
they have decided not to buy.

---

### 4. "Can we do it on a monthly retainer instead?"

**What it usually means.** Either they want to spread the cost, which is
reasonable, or they want somebody permanently available, which is not what we
sell.

**Say:**

> "We do not do retainers, deliberately. A retainer means you pay us every month
> whether you need us or not, and it means I have an incentive to keep the work
> going rather than finish it.
>
> What we do instead is: you pay once for the build, you own it, and then you can
> buy a block of credits and spend them when you actually need something. No
> standing charge. If you go six months without needing me, that costs you
> nothing."

**If it is about cash flow rather than structure:** staged payments against
delivery milestones are a reasonable accommodation and are not a retainer.
[PLACEHOLDER — agree a standard payment schedule, for example a deposit on
signature and the balance on delivery, before quoting the first job.]

**Disqualifier?** Yes, if they will not consider any other structure. This is
structural, not a preference.

---

### 5. "What if it takes you longer than you thought? Who pays for that?"

**What it usually means.** They have been burned by a variable bill before, or
they have heard about someone who was.

**Say:**

> "We do. That is the point of pricing this way. The number I give you is the
> number, and if I have misjudged how long it takes, that is my problem, not
> yours.
>
> The one thing that changes the price is you changing what you want. If that
> happens I will tell you before I do the work, not after."

**Do not say.** Anything that softens this. The fixed number is the single
strongest thing about the commercial model and it should be stated without
qualification.

## Trust and credibility

### 6. "Who else have you worked with? Do you have case studies?"

**What it usually means.** A genuine and reasonable request for evidence. It is
also the question a new business dreads, and the one where the temptation to
embellish is strongest.

**We do not invent clients, case studies or testimonials. Ever.**

**Say:**

> "I am not going to pretend to a client list I do not have. n.abl is new.
>
> What I can show you is what I have built, which is the software this business
> runs on: the site, a client portal with proper access control, an internal
> system, a CRM. I can walk you through any of it and you can judge the work
> rather than the reference.
>
> And I would rather be judged on the diagnosis anyway. Give me twenty minutes on
> your process. If what I tell you about your own business is not worth the
> twenty minutes, you have lost twenty minutes and you will know not to hire me."

**Then, optionally, reduce the risk:**

> "If it helps, we can start with something small. Something with a clear before
> and after, where you can see whether it worked within a fortnight."

**Do not say.** "We have worked with several businesses in your sector." "Our
clients typically see a 40% improvement." Both are inventions and both are the
end of the business if they are ever tested.

**Disqualifier?** No. But a prospect who cannot proceed without references is a
poor fit for phase one and should be recorded and revisited once there is a real
one.

---

### 7. "You are a two-person operation. What happens if you get hit by a bus?"

**What it usually means.** A fair question about continuity, usually asked by
someone who has been left with an orphaned system before.

**Say:**

> "Reasonable question. Three things.
>
> First, you own everything. The code, the data, the accounts. Not licensed from
> us, owned. If we vanish tomorrow, what we built keeps running.
>
> Second, we build with ordinary, well-known tools, not something exotic only we
> understand. Any competent developer can pick it up.
>
> Third, you get the documentation. Written, in plain English, so somebody else
> could take it over. That is part of the job, not an extra."

**Do not say.** Anything that oversells the size of the business. Being small is
a fact and the honesty is worth more than the impression.

---

### 8. "We have tried something like this before and it did not work."

**What it usually means.** The most valuable objection in the list. They have
budget history, an existing belief about why it failed, and a strong preference
not to repeat it. They are also more likely than average to buy, because they
have already accepted that the problem is worth spending money on.

**Say:**

> "Tell me what happened. Genuinely, I want the whole story, including the part
> where it was your fault if it was.
>
> Most of the time these fail for one of three reasons: it was scoped far too big
> and never finished, or it was built for how the business was described rather
> than how it actually works, or it worked and then nobody used it.
>
> Which one was yours?"

**Then scope smaller than instinct suggests.** Whatever felt like a sensible
first project, halve it. The objective of engagement one is a working thing they
can see, not a complete solution.

**Disqualifier?** No, but it is a soft disqualifier if the previous supplier is
still involved, or if the failure is described entirely as somebody else's fault
with no reflection at all.

---

### 9. "Our IT company already does this."

**What it usually means.** Usually a misunderstanding of what we do, because "IT
company" locally means networks, machines, email and support.

**Say:**

> "They probably should keep doing what they do. We are not an IT support
> company. We do not touch your network, your machines or your email.
>
> What we do is the thing your IT company will tell you is not their job: the
> process bit. The spreadsheet that has become a system, the two products that do
> not talk to each other, the job that takes a day a week.
>
> Ask them whether they want to build that. Most of the time they will say no,
> and it is not a criticism of them."

**Do not say.** Anything critical of the incumbent. They may well have the
client's ear, and they are a referral source rather than a competitor.

**Disqualifier?** Only if the IT provider includes development and is contracted
to do it.

## Technology and AI

### 10. "Can I not just use ChatGPT for this?"

**What it usually means.** They have tried a chatbot on a version of the problem
and got a promising result once. They have not tried doing it 400 times a month,
reliably, unattended, with the output landing in a system.

**Say:**

> "Sometimes, honestly, yes — and if that is the answer I will tell you and show
> you how, and it will cost you a training session instead of a project.
>
> Where it stops working is when it has to happen every time without somebody
> driving it, and be right every time. A chatbot is someone sitting next to you
> being helpful. What you are describing needs to run on its own on a Tuesday
> when everyone is out, put the result in the right place, and tell somebody when
> it fails.
>
> And for a lot of this you do not want a model at all. Checking a date is valid
> is not a job for artificial intelligence. It is a job for four lines of code
> that are right every single time and cost nothing to run."

**Disqualifier?** No. Often the opposite: someone who has experimented has
already accepted the premise.

---

### 11. "Is our data going to be fed into an AI?"

**What it usually means.** A real and increasingly common concern, sometimes
prompted by their own customers or their insurer.

**Say:**

> "Most of what we build for you will not involve a model at all. Sorting,
> checking, moving, generating documents — that is ordinary code running on your
> own systems, and nothing leaves.
>
> Where something genuinely needs a model, there are two options. Small models
> that run on a machine, where the data does not go anywhere. Or a hosted model,
> where it does, and in that case I will tell you exactly what is being sent
> before we build it, and you decide.
>
> What I will not do is quietly pipe your customer data through something
> external because it was the easy way to build it."

**Do not say.** Any blanket reassurance that data is "completely secure" or
"never used for training". Say what happens, specifically, for their build.

---

### 12. "Is this going to replace my staff?"

**What it usually means.** Sometimes a worry. Occasionally a hope, and it is
worth knowing which.

**Say:**

> "Almost never, in a business your size. You do not have three people doing data
> entry all day. You have people doing five jobs each, and one of those five is
> the one eating their week.
>
> What actually happens is that the thing that took Thursday afternoon takes
> twenty minutes, and Thursday afternoon goes back to the work you actually
> employed them for."

**If the hope is redundancy**, be straight: a small business rarely saves a whole
salary this way, and promising it is how a project gets judged against a target
it was never going to hit.

---

### 13. "Will it work with [their software]?"

**What it usually means.** A practical question, and frequently the one that
decides the shape of the build.

**Say:**

> "Probably, and I will find out for certain before you pay me anything. Most
> business software has a way in. Some do not, and where that is true I will tell
> you rather than build something fragile that scrapes the screen and breaks
> every time they update it.
>
> What is it, and can you show me what you can already export from it?"

**Do not say.** "Yes, we integrate with everything." Check. An export button is
often enough and is far more robust than a poorly documented interface.

## Fit and timing

### 14. "We are too small for this."

**What it usually means.** They have priced technology projects before and
concluded they are for bigger companies. Usually said by exactly the right kind
of prospect.

**Say:**

> "Small is who this is for. Larger companies have someone in-house for this.
> You do not, which is why the job has ended up on your desk at half past six.
>
> And the arithmetic works better at your size, not worse. If one job is eating a
> day a week of somebody's time, that is a bigger proportion of your business
> than it would be of a company with two hundred staff."

---

### 15. "Now is not a good time. Ring me after [month]."

**What it usually means.** Either a genuine seasonal constraint, which is real in
trades, agriculture, hospitality and accountancy, or a soft no.

**Find out which:**

> "No problem. Is that because you are flat out until then, or because it is not
> a priority? Both are fine answers, I would just rather not pester you about the
> wrong thing."

**If genuinely busy:**

> "Then let us do the twenty minutes now and the work later. The diagnosis does
> not cost you anything and it means that when [month] comes round we are not
> starting from scratch."

**Then set a real, dated follow-up** and log it. The deterministic follow-up timer
in the outreach pipeline exists for exactly this.

**Disqualifier?** No, but a second deferral usually is. Record it and stop.

---

### 16. "Send me some information and I will have a look."

**What it usually means.** Nine times out of ten, a polite exit.

**Say:**

> "I can, but honestly the generic version will not tell you much. What is useful
> is the specific version — what this would look like for your quoting process.
>
> That takes me twenty minutes with you and then I will send you something worth
> reading. Have you got twenty minutes this week?"

**If they still want the generic version**, send it, log it, follow up once, and
move on. Do not build a bespoke document for someone who has not given twenty
minutes.

---

### 17. "I need to think about it. / I need to speak to my business partner."

**What it usually means.** Either a real second decision-maker, which should have
been established during qualification, or an unspoken objection.

**Say:**

> "Of course. Can I ask what you think their first question will be? If it is
> something I can answer now, it saves you having to."

**And:**

> "Is there anything about it that does not sit right with you? I would rather
> hear it now than have you be too polite to tell me."

**Then agree a date.** "I will call you Thursday" is a follow-up. "Let me know"
is not.

**Disqualifier?** If a third person appears who was never mentioned, revisit the
decision-access score in the ICP. This is the failure mode the ICP's decision
access dimension exists to prevent.

---

### 18. "Why not just hire an apprentice or a virtual assistant to do it?"

**What it usually means.** A sensible comparison, honestly considered. Deserves a
straight answer rather than a defensive one.

**Say:**

> "Sometimes that is the right call, and if it is I will say so.
>
> The difference is that a person doing this costs you every month, forever, and
> they leave eventually and take the knowledge with them. A system costs once and
> then keeps working at three in the morning.
>
> The other thing worth thinking about is what you want an apprentice to become.
> If you hire one and their whole job is retyping numbers, they will leave. If
> the retyping is automated and they do the actual work, you might keep them."

---

### 19. "Can we do a trial or a proof of concept first?"

**What it usually means.** Reasonable risk reduction from someone who does not
know us. It can also be a free-work request.

**Say:**

> "Yes, in the sense that the first job should be small enough to be its own
> trial. I would rather build you one real thing that works than a demonstration
> of something that might.
>
> So pick the most annoying single piece of it. That gets built, properly, for a
> price we agree, and it is yours whether or not you ever use me again. If it
> goes badly you have lost a small amount of money and learned something about
> me. If it goes well, we do the next bit."

**Do not say.** Yes to unpaid work. A free pilot is valued at what it costs.

---

### 20. "What if we want to leave? Who owns it?"

**What it usually means.** They have been locked in before, by a website they
cannot edit or a system whose password nobody will hand over.

**Say:**

> "You own it. The code, the data, the accounts, all of it, in your name from the
> start where possible.
>
> If you decide never to speak to me again, everything keeps running and somebody
> else can take it over. There is no licence, nothing switches off, and I do not
> hold anything hostage.
>
> The only thing you stop getting is me."

**This should be said before it is asked**, ideally in the first conversation. It
is one of the strongest differentiators available and it costs nothing.

## The ones that are disqualifiers

Said plainly so nobody talks themselves past them in the moment.

| They say | Verdict |
|---|---|
| "We want to talk about our AI strategy." | **Disqualify.** Technology-led, not problem-led. Ends badly for both sides. |
| "We only work with suppliers on a retainer." | **Disqualify.** Structural. |
| "Could you take equity instead?" | **Disqualify.** |
| "Can you do it for free and we will pay you when it works?" | **Disqualify.** |
| "Our procurement team will send you the questionnaire." | **Disqualify** in phase one. The overhead exceeds the deal. |
| "We need it to give financial/legal/medical advice." | **Disqualify** as specified. Offer to build the system without the advice inside it. |
| "Head office will need to approve it." | **Disqualify** unless head office is in the room. |
| "We are just gathering quotes." (with a fixed written spec) | **Deprioritise.** Price-only competition, no diagnosis, low value. |

### How to disqualify well

The generic version, for when nothing more specific applies:

> "I do not think we are the right fit for this, and I would rather say that now
> than take your money and prove it later. What you actually want is [X], and the
> person to talk to is [Y] if I know one.
>
> If that changes, or if something else comes up that is more our sort of thing,
> do come back."

**A written script for each row of that table is in
[`saying-no.md`](saying-no.md)**, along with the shape of a good no, the nos that
happen mid-project, and the phrases never to use. Read it before a first call,
not during one.

Log the reason in the CRM. A recorded, specific no is an asset. It stops the same
lead being worked twice and it tells us, after fifty of them, which parts of this
profile are wrong.
