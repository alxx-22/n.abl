## 1. What is being assessed

One activity only: **sending a small volume of researched, individually relevant
introductory emails to UK businesses that are corporate subscribers**, about a
service that addresses a problem those businesses plausibly have.

Explicitly **not** covered by this assessment:

- Sole traders, non-LLP partnerships and private individuals. Under PECR these
  are individual subscribers and need consent. Legitimate interests does not
  reach them, and the schema refuses the combination outright
  (`sales_leads_pecr_individual_needs_consent`).
- Telephone marketing. Different rules, different registers (TPS/CTPS), not
  assessed here.
- Any bulk, purchased, scraped or unresearched list.
- Continuing to contact anyone who has objected. That is Article 21(2), it is
  absolute, and it is a suppression row rather than a balancing exercise.

## 2. Purpose — is there a legitimate interest?

Yes. Direct marketing of a business service to other businesses that plausibly
need it. The ICO recognises direct marketing as capable of being a legitimate
interest.

The interest is n.abl's own commercial interest in finding customers. It is not
dressed up as a benefit to the recipient — though where the targeting is done
properly the recipient does get a relevant message rather than an irrelevant one,
which is a genuine, if secondary, point in favour.

## 3. Necessity — is the processing necessary for that purpose?

This limb is where volume decides the answer, and it is the limb most likely to
fail later.

**As assessed:** contacting a researched shortlist of businesses that match a
documented ideal customer profile, where the message references something
specific and true about that business, is necessary to the purpose. There is no
less intrusive way for a new business with no audience to reach a specific small
company that has a specific problem. Advertising does not reach a named
five-person firm in Nottingham with a manual invoicing process.

**What this assessment does not support:** volume in place of targeting.
Contacting several thousand addresses gathered because they were available is a
different activity, and this assessment does not cover it. The distinction is not
rhetorical — it is the thing that makes this limb answerable at all.

**The ceiling this assessment is valid at:** no more than **200 first contacts
per calendar month**, each with a recorded source, a recorded reason for
selection, and human approval before sending. Above that, or with the approval
gates removed, this assessment no longer supports the processing and must be
redone. The figure is deliberately conservative and is set here so that
exceeding it is a visible decision rather than a drift.

## 4. Balance — do the recipient's interests override it?

The factors, honestly weighed.

**Toward n.abl:**

- The data is business contact data, used in a business context, about the
  person's professional role rather than their private life.
- It comes from sources the business itself published or filed publicly: its own
  website, or the Companies House register. Nothing is inferred from private
  sources, and nothing is bought.
- Volume is low and the recipient is selected deliberately, so the message is
  relevant rather than random.
- The recipient is told where their details came from at first contact, and
  opting out is one click and is honoured permanently.
- A business owner receiving a relevant, plainly written email from a local
  supplier would not reasonably find it surprising.

**Toward the recipient:**

- They did not ask to hear from us. That is the whole of the intrusion and it
  should not be minimised.
- Unsolicited email has a cumulative cost that no single sender sees: our one
  message arrives among many.
- Some recipients are one-person limited companies where the "business" address
  is a personal mailbox and the distinction between professional and private is
  thin, even though PECR classifies them as corporate.

**Conclusion:** at the volume, targeting and opt-out handling described above,
the recipient's interests do not override the legitimate interest. That
conclusion is contingent on all three, and it does not survive any of them being
dropped.

The last point above is the reason the tone of the first email matters. A
message that reads as bulk removes the main thing tipping the balance.

## 5. The safeguards this assessment relies on

These are not aspirations. If one of them is not true, the assessment does not
hold.

| Safeguard | Where it is enforced |
|---|---|
| Corporate subscribers only | `sales_leads_pecr_individual_needs_consent` |
| A recorded source and date per lead | `sales_leads_permitted_is_documented` |
| This assessment on file, referenced per lead | `sales_leads_li_needs_assessment` |
| An opt-out route in every message | `marketing_sends_opt_out_required` |
| Objections honoured permanently and never deleted | `marketing_suppression`, append-only |
| Two human approvals before anything sends | `11-outreach/approval-gates.md` |
| Privacy notice at first contact | `privacy_notice_status`, and section 6 |

Six of the seven are enforced by the database rather than by intention. That is
deliberate: an assessment whose safeguards depend on remembering is not a
safeguard.

## 7. What would invalidate this assessment

Any one of these means stop and write a new version:

- Monthly first contacts exceed 200, or the approval gates are removed.
- The source mix changes — buying a list, scraping at volume, or using a source
  whose terms forbid it.
- The audience widens beyond corporate subscribers.
- The message stops being individually relevant and becomes a template sent wide.
- Complaint volume becomes non-trivial, or the ICO publishes guidance that moves
  the position.
- The channel changes: this covers electronic mail only.
