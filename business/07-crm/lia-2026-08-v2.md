## 0. Why there is a v2

`LIA-2026-08-v1` capped first contacts at 200 per calendar month and excluded
"any bulk, purchased, scraped or unresearched list". Sourcing has since changed:
the candidate pool is now built from whole published register files — the
Companies House Free Company Data Product and the FSA Food Hygiene Rating Scheme
open data — giving roughly 74,500 distinct businesses in our territory rather
than a hand-built shortlist of a few hundred.

Section 7 of v1 says that is an invalidating change. It is, and this is the
redone assessment rather than an edit to the old one.

The commercial pressure behind the change is real and is stated openly: a new
business with no audience needs enough top-of-funnel to survive. That pressure
is a reason to reassess honestly. It is not a reason to reach a particular
answer, and where the answer below is no, it says no.

## 1. The thing v1 got wrong, and the thing it got right

**Right:** volume cannot substitute for targeting, and the necessity limb is
where a high-volume claim fails. That reasoning survives intact and is carried
into section 4 below.

**Wrong, or at least imprecise:** v1 treated "volume" as one number and set one
ceiling on it. But the size of the *candidate pool* and the size of the *contact
programme* are different quantities with different legal footings, and v1 blurred
them. Holding a copy of a register that the state publishes for download is a
materially different act from sending someone an email. A large pool that is
filtered hard produces *better* targeting than a small pool that is not — you
pick the fifty best-fit businesses instead of the fifty you happened to find.

More importantly, v1 assessed a single audience where there are three, and only
one of them actually needs a legitimate interests assessment at all.

## 2. Three audiences, not one

The register data does not identify people. Companies House returns a *company*;
the FSA returns a *premises*. A person only enters the record at the enrichment
step, and whether one does decides which regime applies.

**Tier A — corporate subscriber, generic role address, no named individual held.**
A limited company, LLP or PLC, contacted at `info@`, `hello@`, `enquiries@` or
similar, with no natural person's name, personal address or personal contact
details in the record.

- *PECR:* regulation 22, which requires consent for unsolicited marketing email,
  restricts sending to **individual subscribers**. A limited company is a
  corporate subscriber. Regulation 22 does not apply. Regulation 23 does, and
  requires that we not disguise our identity and that we give a valid address to
  opt out — both of which we do.
- *UK GDPR:* a generic role inbox at a company, held alongside company-level
  register data, is not personal data. No living individual is identified or
  identifiable from it. UK GDPR is not engaged.
- **Conclusion: no legitimate interests assessment is required for Tier A,
  because there is no personal data to balance.** This document still governs it,
  for the reasons in section 6.

**Tier B — corporate subscriber, named individual.**
The same companies, but where we hold a director's or manager's name, or a
personal-form address such as `firstname@`.

- *PECR:* still a corporate subscriber, so regulation 22 still does not apply.
- *UK GDPR:* engaged. The name is personal data about a living individual in
  their professional capacity. A lawful basis is needed and legitimate interests
  is the one relied on. **This is what v1 assessed and what sections 3 to 5
  below reassess.**

**Tier C — individual subscribers.**
Sole traders, non-LLP partnerships in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, and
private individuals.

- *PECR:* individual subscribers. Regulation 22 applies in full. Consent or the
  soft opt-in is required, and we have neither.
- **Excluded, exactly as in v1.** The schema refuses the combination
  (`sales_leads_pecr_individual_needs_consent`).

**This split is not a device for getting to a bigger number.** It is the actual
structure of PECR, and it is why the ICO's own direct marketing guidance says
business-to-business email to corporate subscribers does not need consent. It
does, however, put a hard obligation on the pipeline: **a candidate whose
subscriber type is unknown is Tier C until proved otherwise.** See section 5.

## 3. Purpose — is there a legitimate interest?

Unchanged from v1, and it applies to Tier B. Direct marketing of a business
service to other businesses that plausibly need it. The interest is n.abl's own
commercial interest in finding customers; it is not dressed up as a benefit to
the recipient.

## 4. Necessity — is the processing necessary for that purpose?

This is the limb v1 said would fail first, and the analysis has to be done per
tier rather than in aggregate.

**Holding the pool.** Necessary, and close to unavoidable. Companies House and
the FSA publish these registers as whole files. There is no per-company lookup
that would let us hold less: the Companies House bulk product is the only free
route to the register, and taking the file and filtering it locally to our
territory holds *less* about the businesses we do not want than a query API
would disclose about the ones we do. The pool is filtered to Nottinghamshire and
the two Warwickshire districts on download, so 5.7 million company records were
read and 66,925 retained.

**Contacting Tier A.** The necessity limb belongs to UK GDPR, and UK GDPR is not
engaged. There is no processing of personal data to justify. What constrains
Tier A volume is regulation 23, deliverability, and whether the message is any
good — not a balancing test. Section 6 sets a ceiling anyway, and says why.

**Contacting Tier B.** v1's reasoning stands and is not softened. Contacting a
researched shortlist where the message references something specific and true
about that business is necessary; sending several thousand people the same
template because their addresses were available is a different activity and this
assessment does not cover it either.

What *has* changed is the quality of the shortlist. A 74,500-row pool carrying
SIC codes, incorporation dates, trading addresses and hygiene ratings supports a
better-founded "something specific and true" than a hand-built list did — the
specific thing can be derived from the register entry rather than invented. That
raises what the necessity limb will bear. It does not remove it.

**Where the answer is no.** Sending an identical message to every reachable
address in the pool fails this limb, and would fail it at any pool size. It is
not covered by this assessment and section 8 lists it as invalidating.

## 5. The tier gate, and why it is the load-bearing safeguard

Everything above depends on knowing which tier a candidate is in. Getting that
wrong in the permissive direction means emailing a sole trader without consent,
which is a PECR breach regardless of how good the assessment is.

The gate is therefore **fail-closed**, in the same pattern as
`marketing_send_allowed`: unknown is not permission.

| Evidence | Tier | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Companies House record, category Private/Public Limited Company, LLP, PLC | A or B | Corporate subscriber on the face of the register |
| FSA record **with** a confirmed Companies House match | A or B | Corporate subscriber, corroborated by two registers |
| FSA record with **no** company match | **C — blocked** | May well be a sole trader; the FSA does not record legal form |
| Any record where subscriber type is unmapped | **C — blocked** | Unknown is not permission |
| Named individual present in the record | B, not A | A name is personal data, whatever the company is |

Of the 74,494 candidates merged on 21 August 2026, 66,922 carry a Companies
House record and are corporate subscribers on the face of the register. 7,572
are FSA records with no company match and are blocked until one is found. The
gate costs us roughly a tenth of the pool, and that is the correct trade.

Two further points that the tiering must not be allowed to obscure:

- **A one-person limited company is a corporate subscriber whose inbox is a
  personal mailbox.** PECR classifies it as corporate and that classification is
  correct in law, but the intrusion is closer to a personal one. v1 made this
  point and it is not weakened by anything in v2.
- **A registered office is often an accountant's.** Reaching the business at all
  is why the merge prefers a trading address, and a candidate we can only reach
  via a third party's address is not one we should be emailing.

## 6. Balance, and the ceiling

For Tier B the factors are as v1 set them out and are not restated. The
conclusion is the same: at a volume where the message stays specific, with a
recorded source and an honoured opt-out, the recipient's interests do not
override the legitimate interest.

**The ceilings this assessment is valid at, per calendar month:**

| Tier | Ceiling | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| A — corporate, generic address, no named person | **2,000 first contacts** | Not a data protection limit; see below |
| B — corporate, named individual | **400 first contacts** | Legitimate interests balance, doubled from v1 on the strength of the sourcing |
| C — individual subscribers | **0** | No consent, no soft opt-in |

The Tier A figure is not derived from UK GDPR, because UK GDPR is not engaged.
It is set for three reasons, and they are stated so that later argument about it
starts from the truth:

1. **Regulation 23 and honesty in the message.** A volume at which we stop being
   able to say something true and specific about each recipient is a volume at
   which the programme becomes the thing this document refuses to authorise.
   2,000 a month is roughly what two people can send while still meaning it.
2. **The classification could be wrong.** The tier gate is evidence-based, not
   certain. A ceiling limits the damage of a systematic misclassification to a
   recoverable size.
3. **It is a decision, not a drift.** Same reasoning as v1's 200: exceeding it
   should require someone to write a v3, not to change a config value.

Tier B doubles rather than multiplies. The sourcing improvement is real but it
is an improvement in *selection*, and the balance in v1 turned on the recipient
receiving something that does not read as bulk. Four hundred well-founded
approaches a month is a genuine increase; four thousand would be the activity
v1 correctly refused.

**Combined, this assessment supports up to 2,400 first contacts a month against
v1's 200.** That is a twelvefold increase, and it comes from correcting a
misclassification in v1 rather than from relaxing a judgement in it.

## 7. The safeguards this assessment relies on

As v1, plus the tier gate. If one of these is not true, the assessment does not
hold.

| Safeguard | Where it is enforced |
|---|---|
| Corporate subscribers only, unknown treated as individual | `sales_leads_pecr_individual_needs_consent`, and section 5 |
| A recorded source and date per lead | `sales_leads_permitted_is_documented` |
| This assessment on file, referenced per lead | `sales_leads_li_needs_assessment` |
| An opt-out route in every message | `marketing_sends_opt_out_required` |
| Objections honoured permanently and never deleted | `marketing_suppression`, append-only |
| Two human approvals before anything sends | `11-outreach/approval-gates.md` |
| Privacy notice at first contact | `privacy_notice_status` |
| Monthly ceilings per tier | `marketing_ceiling_guard`, `202608210001` |

All eight are enforced by the database rather than by intention. The ceiling was
the one that nearly was not: an earlier draft of this section listed it as
outstanding, and a ceiling that lives only in this document is a number someone
has to remember, which v1 correctly said is not a safeguard at all.

How it works, so that the number in section 6 can be checked against the code
rather than trusted:

- `marketing_tier(lead)` returns C, B or A by the rules in section 2, and
  returns C for anything it cannot place. Unknown is not permission.
- Every row in `marketing_sends` is stamped at insert with its tier, whether it
  was a first contact, and whether it spent allowance. Stamped rather than
  derived, because a lead's tier changes the moment we learn a director's name
  and a count that moved retroactively would be no count at all.
- `marketing_ceiling_guard` refuses the first contact that would cross the
  ceiling for its tier, in the calendar month.
- A recipient who **consented** is exempt from the ceiling and spends none of
  it. A ceiling limits how far we push ourselves onto people who did not ask;
  someone who asked is not that. Counting them would let a consented audience
  crowd out the legitimate interests one.

`scripts/check-compliance-schema.mjs` asserts all of this against a real
PostgreSQL, including that the 401st tier B first contact in a month is refused
and that last month's contacts do not count against this one.
`scripts/mutate-compliance-checks.mjs` then reintroduces each bug in turn and
confirms the assertion covering it goes red, because a suite that cannot fail
proves nothing.

## 8. What would invalidate this assessment

Any one of these means stop and write a new version:

- Monthly first contacts exceed the ceiling for their tier, or the approval gates
  are removed.
- The tier gate stops failing closed, or unknown subscriber types start being
  treated as corporate.
- The message stops being individually relevant and becomes one template sent
  wide. This is the one most likely to happen quietly.
- The source mix changes — buying a list, taking data from a site whose terms
  forbid it, or using a source that is not published as open data by the body
  that holds it.
- The audience widens beyond corporate subscribers.
- Named individuals start being held for Tier A records, which makes them Tier B.
- Complaint volume becomes non-trivial, or the ICO publishes guidance that moves
  the position.
- The channel changes: this covers electronic mail only. Post, telephone and
  social channels have different rules and are not assessed here.
