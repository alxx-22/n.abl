# Permission, testimonials, and the anonymised route

Getting a result is half of proof. Being allowed to talk about it is the other
half, and it is the half that is lost by asking at the wrong moment.

---

## 1. When to ask

| Moment | Ask for |
|---|---|
| **Scope of work, before work starts** | Permission *in principle* to write up the work, subject to their approval of the wording. Costs nothing to include, and makes the later ask a formality |
| **At handover** | Nothing yet. Let it work first |
| **One week after handover** | The testimonial. This is the peak of goodwill: the relief is fresh and they have just watched something start working |
| **One full cycle after handover** | The figures, and permission for the specific published wording |
| **Any time they say something good** | Write it down verbatim, with the date. Ask to use it later |

**The one-week window is the one people miss.** Gratitude decays. A month later
the improvement is normal, it is simply how the business works now, and nobody
feels moved to say anything about it. Ask while it still feels like a change.

---

## 2. Asking in principle, in the scope of work

Wording to fold into `04-legal/scope-of-work-template.md`, so it arrives as a
routine clause rather than a favour:

> **Talking about this work.** With your written approval, we may describe this
> project — what the problem was, what we built, and what changed — on our
> website or in a proposal to another business. Nothing is published without you
> seeing the exact wording first and agreeing to it. You can decline, or ask to
> be described without being named, at any point and for any reason. Declining
> changes nothing about the work or the price.

The last sentence is not padding. Without it, a client can reasonably wonder
whether saying no costs them something, and the answer must be visibly no.

---

## 3. Asking for the testimonial

Do not send a form. Ask a person a question.

> "We're at the point where I'd normally ask for a testimonial, and I never know
> how to do it without it being awkward — so: would you be up for writing a
> couple of sentences about how it's gone? Anything honest is useful, including
> the bits that were harder than expected."

Three things that make this work:

1. **Naming the awkwardness removes it.**
2. **Inviting the negative** makes the positive believable, and occasionally
   produces something genuinely useful about the delivery process.
3. **"A couple of sentences"** is a small ask. "A testimonial" sounds like
   homework.

### If they say yes but never write it

Common, and not a refusal. Offer the low-effort route:

> "Easier option — you said on the phone that *[verbatim thing they said]*.
> Would you be happy for us to use that, as is? Or edit it however you like."

**Never draft it for them and ask them to approve it.** An approved draft is our
words in their mouth, and it reads like it. Quoting something they actually said
is a different thing entirely: those are their words, and the only decision they
are making is whether we may repeat them.

### If they say no

Say thank you, and mean it. Ask whether an anonymised version would be all
right. Do not ask twice.

---

## 4. Recording permission

Permission that is not written down does not exist. For each published item:

| Field | |
|---|---|
| Client and contact | Who agreed |
| What exactly was agreed | The specific wording, pasted in full |
| Where it may appear | Website, proposals, both, or a named limit |
| Named or anonymised | And if anonymised, the agreed descriptor |
| Date agreed | |
| Evidence | The email, saved. A reply saying "yes that's fine" to a message containing the exact wording |
| Withdrawal | Blank until it happens. If it does, it happens immediately and without discussion |

**Permission is per-use, not general.** Agreement to a case study on the website
is not agreement to use their logo on a deck, and is not agreement to a
different form of words next year. If the wording changes materially, ask again.

**Withdrawal is honoured immediately.** No negotiation, no "we'll take it down
at the next release". A client who withdraws permission and finds their name
still published a month later is a reference call that goes badly for years.

---

## 5. The anonymised route

Most small businesses are happy to be described and reluctant to be named,
especially where the work touches something they would rather competitors not
know about. Anonymised proof is close to as persuasive as named proof, and it is
much easier to obtain — so offer it early rather than treating it as a
consolation.

**The format:**

> **A [size] [sector] business in [region]**
> — e.g. "An eight-person building firm in Nottinghamshire"

Rules:

- The descriptor must be **agreed with the client**, in those words.
- It must be **general enough not to identify them**. In a small town, "the only
  vehicle-hire firm in X" is not anonymous. If the sector and region together
  narrow it to a handful of businesses, broaden one of them.
- **Figures still require sign-off.** Anonymity is not a licence to be loose with
  numbers; if anything it makes accuracy more important, because nobody outside
  can check.
- The work described must still be **truthfully described**. Anonymised does not
  mean composite. **Never merge two engagements into one story.** That is a
  fabricated client wearing a disclaimer.

---

## 6. Logos

Do not use a client's logo without explicit, separate, written permission naming
that use. A logo on a website implies endorsement and, in some sectors, a
partnership. It is a bigger ask than a case study and should be treated as one.

Until there is a real set of them, there is no logo wall. An empty or
near-empty one advertises exactly the thing it is trying to conceal.
