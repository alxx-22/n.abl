# The ROI worksheet

The questions to ask on a call to fill in the arithmetic.

This is a script, not a form. It is held in your head and filled in as the client
talks. Nobody is impressed by being read a questionnaire, and a business owner
who feels audited stops giving you real numbers.

**Status: never used on a real call.** Every question below is untested. Expect
to rewrite parts of it after the first three.

The arithmetic it feeds is the master plan, §3:

```
  current monthly cost of the problem
– expected monthly cost after implementation
= monthly value created
× 12
= first-year value

price = a fraction of first-year value
```

---

## Before you start

Five rules that decide whether the worksheet produces a number or a fiction.

**1. This is for category A only.** Efficiency solutions: a process the business
already does, made cheaper, faster or better. If the answer to "did you already
do this, just worse?" is no, stop. It is a capability job, it is fixed price, and
there is no before-figure to measure. See `quote-template.md`, template B.

**2. Their numbers, not yours.** You ask. They answer. You write down what they
said. You never fill a gap with a plausible figure, and you never say "most
businesses find it takes about…". The moment you supply the estimate, you own it.

**3. Without a count there is no price.** A process nobody can put a frequency or
a duration on cannot be priced on value. That is not a reason to guess; it is a
finding, and it usually means the first piece of work is measurement.

**4. Round the saving down and the residual up.** Every ambiguity resolves
against us. A client who gets a slightly better outcome than the quote said is a
reference. A client who gets a slightly worse one is a complaint, and the
complaint is about a number we wrote.

**5. Do not ask for their budget.** Not early, not late. The budget question is
what you ask instead of doing the arithmetic, and it invites them to price the
job by what they can bear rather than by what it is worth.

## Part 1 — Find the process

You are looking for one task, done repeatedly, by named people, that somebody can
describe in terms of how long it takes.

| # | Ask | What you are listening for |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "What is the job that annoys you most?" | The opening. Annoyance is a better signal than cost, because people track it more accurately. |
| 2 | "Talk me through it from the beginning. What starts it?" | A trigger. An email arrives, an order lands, a Monday comes round. |
| 3 | "Then what happens? And then?" | The steps. Keep saying "and then" until they reach the end. |
| 4 | "Where does it end? How do you know it is finished?" | A boundary. Without one you will scope the whole department. |
| 5 | "What is on either side of it?" | Adjacent processes. Note them, do not price them. |
| 6 | "Who else touches it?" | The denominator is people × time, and people get forgotten. |
| 7 | "What happens if it does not get done this week?" | Whether it matters. Some annoying tasks cost nothing to skip. |

**Write the process down in one sentence before moving on**, and say it back to
them. If they correct you, you have just avoided quoting for the wrong job.

## Part 2 — The current cost

This is the before-figure. It is built from three components, in order of how
reliable they are: time, then errors, then delay. Most jobs are time only, and
that is fine.

### 2a. Time

| # | Ask | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | "How often does it happen? Every day, every week, every order?" | Get a frequency you can multiply. "Whenever it comes in" is not one. |
| 9 | "And roughly how long each time?" | Minutes, honestly stated, beat a monthly total pulled out of the air. |
| 10 | "Is that the whole thing, or just your part?" | Catches the invisible second person. |
| 11 | "Who does it? Are they always the same person?" | Names, and roles. The rate depends on the role. |
| 12 | "Does anyone check it afterwards?" | Checking time is time. |
| 13 | "Is it worse at month end, or in season?" | Peaks. Price on the ordinary month, mention the peak. |

Convert to hours per month, in front of them, out loud:

> "So that is about twenty minutes, three times a week — call it four hours a
> month, plus the hour Jane spends checking. Five hours. Does that sound right or
> does it sound low?"

**Always ask whether it sounds low.** People underestimate recurring tasks and
they correct you cheerfully when invited to.

### 2b. The loaded cost of an hour

The rate is not the wage. It is what an hour of that person costs the business,
which is gross pay plus employer National Insurance, plus pension, plus the fact
that holiday and sick leave are paid out of the same salary for fewer worked
hours, plus the desk, the software and the equipment.

| # | Ask | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 14 | "Who does this, and roughly what does that role cost you a year, all in?" | Some owners answer straight away. Some will not, and that is their right. |
| 15 | "Shall we just use a loaded figure of £20 an hour and adjust it if you think it is wrong?" | The fallback. It is the canonical figure, it is defensible, and it gives them something to push against. |
| 16 | "Is it you doing it?" | If the owner does it, the real cost is what they would otherwise be doing. Say that; do not try to price it. |

Two things to hold:

- **£20 an hour is the canonical illustrative loaded cost**, used in the master
  plan, on the website and in `worked-examples.md`. Use it when a client cannot
  or will not give you theirs, and label it as an assumption on the quote.
- **Never inflate the rate to make the job look better.** The arithmetic is
  checkable in front of them, and a rate they do not recognise destroys the whole
  page.

### 2c. Mistakes, if there are any

Only include this when the client volunteers a number they can stand behind.

| # | Ask | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 17 | "How often does it go wrong?" | A count per month or per quarter. |
| 18 | "When it does, what does putting it right cost you?" | Refunds, redelivery, a credit note, an afternoon. Their figure. |
| 19 | "Has it ever cost you a customer?" | Do not price this. Note it and let them price it in their own head. |

### 2d. Delay, if it is real

| # | Ask | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | "Does anything wait on this?" | Invoices going out late is a cash-flow cost, not a labour cost. |
| 21 | "What does that waiting cost you?" | If they cannot say, leave it out of the arithmetic and mention it in words. |

**When in doubt, leave a component out.** A quote built on time alone, with the
errors described in a sentence and not counted, is stronger than a quote with a
large number nobody can defend.

## Part 3 — The after picture

This is the part people skip, and skipping it is how a quote becomes a
disappointment. You are not estimating a perfect outcome. You are estimating
**what is left**.

| # | Ask | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 22 | "Of those five hours, how much is actual judgement, and how much is moving data about?" | The judgement stays. The moving is what gets automated. |
| 23 | "Would you want to check the output, at least at first?" | Yes. They always should. Checking time is residual time. |
| 24 | "What are the awkward cases, the ones that are never standard?" | Exceptions stay manual, and there are always more than they first say. |
| 25 | "If this ran itself, what would still need a person once a month?" | Their own description of the residual, in their words. |

Then state the residual as a number and get agreement:

> "So realistically this does not go to zero. Call it an hour a month of checking
> and awkward cases. Everything I am about to say is built on that, not on it
> disappearing."

**Never quote a residual of zero.** No process goes to zero. A stated residual is
also the strongest credibility move available on a first call, because everyone
else in the market says "fully automated".

---

## Part 4 — Evidence and confidence

Before the arithmetic is worth anything, ask what the figures are made of.

| # | Ask | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 26 | "Is that measured, or is that your sense of it?" | Both are usable. They are recorded differently. |
| 27 | "Could you show me? Could we look at last month?" | The best possible outcome. A screen share beats every estimate. |
| 28 | "Has anyone tried to fix this before?" | Tells you why it is still broken, and what not to propose. |
| 29 | "What would have to be true for this to be worth doing?" | Their own threshold, in their own words, before you name a price. |

Record a confidence level against the worksheet: **measured**, **estimated by the
person doing it**, or **estimated by someone else**. Third-hand estimates get
rounded down harder.

## Part 5 — The arithmetic

Do this in front of them if the call is going well. On paper, or on the screen.
Being checkable is most of why it works.

```
Task:                    ____________________________________

Frequency:               ______ times per ______
Duration each time:      ______ minutes
People involved:         ______

Current hours per month:                    ______  h
Loaded cost per hour:                    £  ______
Current monthly labour cost:             £  ______      (A)

Errors per month:        ______  at £ ______ each
Current monthly error cost:              £  ______      (B)

CURRENT MONTHLY COST            (A + B) = £  ______      (C)

Residual hours per month after:             ______  h
Residual monthly labour cost:            £  ______      (D)
Residual monthly error cost:             £  ______      (E)

EXPECTED MONTHLY COST AFTER     (D + E) = £  ______      (F)

MONTHLY VALUE CREATED             (C – F) = £  ______    (G)
FIRST-YEAR VALUE                   (G × 12) = £  ______  (H)   ← round down

Implementation price:                    £  ______      (P)
Payback period               (P ÷ G) = ______ months

Confidence:   measured / estimated by doer / estimated by other
Evidence seen:           ____________________________________
```

### Turning H into P

The price is a fraction of first-year value. The canonical example anchors the
band: £2,400 of first-year value supports an implementation of roughly £800 to
£1,500, so the band runs from about a third to about three-fifths, with the
midpoint £1,200 sitting at half.

| First-year value (H) | Illustrative band | Midpoint | Payback at midpoint |
|---|---|---|---|
| £2,400 | £800 – £1,500 | £1,200 | 6 months |

**The house fraction is [PLACEHOLDER — set it after the first three real
quotes].** Until then, use the canonical band and write down why you landed where
you landed. What moves the number within the band: how much of the build is
reusable, how much of it is Class 1 code, how confident the figures are, and how
much of the residual is genuinely uncertain.

What must never move it: how long you personally expect to spend. That is the
internal cost line, it goes in the job record, and it is never sold.

## Part 6 — What to do with the answer

**The arithmetic supports a comfortable price.** Proceed. Write the quote from
`quote-template.md`, template A, with a matching scope of work from `04-legal`.

**The arithmetic is marginal.** First-year value is real but small. Options, in
order: fold it into a larger piece of work in the same business, defer it and
sell it later as credits, or show them the numbers and say that on these figures
you would leave it for now. Saying that is the cheapest reputation you will ever
buy.

**The arithmetic does not support any build.** Show them, on the call. See
`worked-examples.md`, §4 — the phrasing matters, and the rule is that the
numbers do the refusing rather than us. "This isn't worth building" is a verdict
on their problem; "here is the arithmetic, and on these figures I'd leave it" is
the same information with the decision left where it belongs. A business shown
honestly why their annoying job does not pay back remembers who showed them.

**There is no before-figure at all.** It is a capability job. Fixed price, scope
in writing, no ROI arithmetic anywhere on the document.

**Nobody knows any of the numbers.** The first piece of work is measurement, not
automation. That is a real conversation, not a failure, but do not price a build
on figures that were invented on the call to keep the meeting moving.

## After the call

- [ ] Write the worksheet up the same day, while the phrasing is still fresh.
- [ ] Record the confidence level and what evidence you actually saw.
- [ ] Round the first-year value down before it goes anywhere near a quote.
- [ ] Note the residual in the client's own words. It goes in the quote verbatim.
- [ ] Store the worksheet with the quote number, so any figure on the quote can be
      traced to a sentence somebody said.
- [ ] Record the expected internal cost of delivery for `16-finance`, in the job
      record, not in the quote.
- [ ] If the answer was no, write down why. Three of those are worth more than
      three quotes for shaping who to talk to next.

---

## Things this worksheet must never be used to do

**Justify a number already decided.** If the price comes first and the arithmetic
is assembled to reach it, the arithmetic is decoration and the client will feel
it.

**Promise a saving.** Every figure is expected, projected, based on figures the
client supplied. `04-legal` is explicit: value pricing is not a promise of value,
and no quote guarantees an outcome.

**Price a capability job.** No before, no subtraction, no worksheet.

**Produce an hourly rate.** Not even privately, not even as a check on whether
the price is sensible. Our own hours are tracked internally and never sold,
because hourly pricing punishes us for getting faster.
